## Self-Reflection Loops in AI: Worth the Cost?

이 제목은 아래 게시물에서 AI가 요약한 것입니다.

Finally a good paper testing whether self-reflection loops are worth it. Setup: Seven methods, open models at 1.5B, 3B and 7B, two math benchmarks, 150 questions each. Every generated token counted, including the ones spent on critiques, reflections, debate turns and checking. Each method compared against repeated sampling at its own measured cost, paired by question, with bootstrap intervals and multiplicity correction. All 36 comparisons came back with no reliable win for any method. Ten are reliably worse, and every one of those is a method where the model inspects its own output. All 18 self-inspection comparisons are negative. Self-Refine and a forced Reflexion sit 3.6 to 10.1 points below the baseline at 7B. Reflexion as published never triggered its own retry on the smallest model. It judged itself correct every time and quietly became a single chain of thought. Worth knowing before you add another critique step to your agent loop.

Reflexion quietly becoming a single chain of thought is the finding I would post on the wall. A self-check that never fires does not look like a broken gate, it looks like a passing one, and nothing in the trace tells you the difference. The useful reading is that verification only buys you something when it comes from outside the thing being verified: a test, a regression suite, a checker carrying its own ground truth. - attention is just a weighted lookup every token asks a question (query), every token offers an answer (key), and the match between them decides how much of each token's content (value) gets pulled in. no recurrence, no fixed window. every position looks at every other position, all at once, and learns which ones actually matter for it. take "the cat sat on the mat." the token "sat" doesn't process the sentence left to right. it directly asks: who's relevant to me right now? mostly itself, a good chunk of "cat," barely anything from "the" or "on." that's the whole trick behind transformers. dot product the query against every key, softmax the scores so they sum to 1, use those as weights on the value vectors, sum it up. repeat across many heads, many layers, and the model builds up a surprisingly rich sense of context. what makes it fast is that it's just matrix multiplication. what makes it powerful is that the weighting is learned, not hardcoded.

- Our test-generating model hallucinated fake method names 67% of the time. The fix wasn't a bigger model. It was showing the model what it was actually mocking. When we started, the generated pytest tests looked right: proper structure, sensible assertions. But the mocks called methods that didn't exist. Pass rate sat at 33%. The instinct is to reach for a larger model. That wouldn't have helped. No model, at any size, knows the method signatures in your codebase at inference time. So we changed the context, not the weights. Two stages: import the real modules and read their signatures with inspect, then fall back to an ast scan of local files for anything not installed. The model finally saw the true interfaces it had to mock against. Same weights, new context: - mock hallucination: 67% → 3% - test pass rate: 33% → 97% The lesson I keep coming back to: fine-tuning teaches a model the shape of a good output. It cannot teach it the state of your repo today. Two different problems, and only one is solved by scaling up. How often, in your experience, is a context problem misdiagnosed as a model-quality problem?

- When the output disappoints, the instinct is to add a rule. I want to talk you out of that instinct — or at least bound it. There's an inversion point past which more specification degrades output. Past it, the model starts optimizing for rule-compliance over quality, and you get technically-obedient, architecturally tone-deaf code. More capable models hit that inversion sooner and more sharply. Practitioner ceiling: roughly 500 instructions before instruction-following measurably erodes. Cursor's own docs recommend keeping rule sets under 500 lines. Past that, the next rule is making it worse, not better — you just can't feel it, because the model is complying with the letter of what you wrote. The second half of this is an enforcement distinction most teams get backwards: A rule in your [CLAUDE.md](https://www.linkedin.com/redir/redirect?url=http%3A%2F%2FCLAUDE%2Emd&urlhash=onXc&trk=public_post-text) is probabilistic — the agent usually follows it. A hook, a linter, a pre-commit check is deterministic — the agent cannot bypass it. So "never run \`rm -rf\`" sitting in a context file is not a safeguard. It's a wish. Critical, irreversible constraints belong in deterministic enforcement. Stylistic preferences are fine as probabilistic guidance. Mismatching the enforcement type to the cost of the violation is a quiet, common source of production failures. More rules feel like more control. Past the ceiling, they're less.

- There are two versions of chart reading, one sophisticated, one dangerous. They look almost identical from the outside. Pattern matching is the surface version. The chart looks like a flag. Flags go up. Buy the flag. There's nothing wrong with it as a foundation, but applied without context, it produces trades that technically match a historical structure while missing every meaningful condition that made the structure valid. A flag on a micro-cap stock in a thin afternoon market is not the same trade as a flag on a large-cap name in the morning session. The shape is the same. The trade is completely different. Pattern recognition is the deeper version. It asks not just what the shape looks like, but why the shape appears in the first place. What are market participants doing to produce it? What conditions must be present for the logic behind the pattern to hold? A trader with genuine pattern recognition can see a setup that doesn't match any textbook pattern and know it's valid, because they understand the underlying mechanics well enough to read the picture beyond the shape. Building from matching to recognition is the work of years. It happens through repetition combined with hypothesis testing. You see a pattern, form a theory about why it works, track the conditions under which it succeeds and fails, and over time, develop an understanding that goes beyond template-fitting. The pattern is no longer a shape you're looking for. It's a story about supply and demand you're learning to read. If your current trading is mostly pattern-matching, that's where most traders begin. The step up is to start asking why behind every setup you take, not just what. The why is where the real edge lives.
